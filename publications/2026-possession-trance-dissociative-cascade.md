## Core Idea

This paper introduces the Adaptive Engram Migration Hypothesis (AEMH) as applied to possession trance — a phenomenon designated as Dissociative Trance Disorder in the ICD-11, historically documented across numerous cultural settings, and known in the Indonesian-Javanese tradition as *kesurupan*.

The central question is not whether the brain can generate these states — the evidence that it can is sufficient — but *why* it does so in specific cultural forms, and whether that specificity is itself informative about the underlying mechanism.

The AEMH proposes that acute psychosocial overload triggers a structured dissociative cascade in which distress-associated memory-affect patterns — referred to by deliberate metaphor as *engrams* — become transiently decoupled from the primary autobiographical self-network and are expressed through a secondary identity schema that is culturally pre-formed and socially sanctioned.

## Why This Matters

The hypothesis proposes that the mechanism is *adaptive* in the ecological sense: it allows intolerable psychological load to be discharged through a culturally legible channel, without requiring the primary identity to sustain the full weight of that experience.

Rather than treating possession trance purely as pathology, the paper opens the possibility that the cultural scripting of dissociative states may reflect a community-level protective mechanism — one that has persisted across cultures because it functions.

**Clinical implications:** If the hypothesis is supported, the implications concern not only dissociative trance but the broader question of how cultural environments shape the neurobiology of emotional regulation under extreme stress.

**Ethical architecture:** The paper explicitly addresses how to conduct this research without reproducing colonial or psychiatric over-pathologizing frameworks.
